Timetabling

What challenges do university staff face without timetable automation?

Manual timetabling means weeks of data-chasing, clash-fixing and rework for staff. The key challenges, and how IIM Calcutta cut timetable creation time by 70%.

By Arun Korupolu

On this page

  1. What does building a timetable by hand actually involve?
  2. What challenges do staff face without timetable automation?
  3. What changes when timetabling is automated?
  4. What should a programme office do before automating?
  5. Frequently asked questions

The short answer: without IT and automation systems, university staff build timetables by hand from scattered data, find clashes only after students complain, and rework the schedule every time a faculty member, room or enrolment changes. The result is long hours, frequent errors, rigid schedules and a process that stops working as the institution grows. Automation hands that work to a system: at IIM Calcutta, it cut the time spent creating timetables by 70%, from weeks to days.

This post looks at timetabling from the programme office's side: what building a timetable by hand really involves, the challenges staff run into, and what changes once the process is automated.

What does building a timetable by hand actually involve?

Picture a programme office preparing the timetable for a new term without a scheduling system. The work usually runs like this:

  1. Gathering the data. The course list, sections, faculty availability and room list arrive in separate emails and spreadsheets, often in different formats, and some of it is out of date.
  2. Building a first draft. Someone slots courses into a grid, trying to keep faculty preferences and room sizes in mind at the same time.
  3. Guessing the clashes. Without data on which courses students take together, clash checking relies on memory and last year's timetable.
  4. Circulating and revising. Drafts go to faculty and area chairs. Objections come back. The grid is redone.
  5. Absorbing registration. Once students register, new clashes appear that nobody predicted.
  6. Handling changes mid-term. A faculty member is travelling, a room goes out of use, a section is oversubscribed. Each change ripples through the grid.
  7. Telling everyone. Every update has to reach students and faculty, and someone has to check that it did.

Most of this is repeated every term. At IIM Calcutta, manual timetabling required constant back-and-forth between faculty, administration and students, juggling demand predictions, conflict avoidance and faculty constraints without any unified system. Read the IIM Calcutta case study.

What challenges do staff face without timetable automation?

1. Time-consuming manual work and heavy workloads

Creating and managing timetables by hand takes a great deal of time. Staff handle large volumes of data, including course schedules, room availability and instructor preferences. The work is repetitive, and the workload that builds up around it can lead to burnout and lower job satisfaction.

2. A high risk of human error

Manual entry makes scheduling conflicts, double bookings and missed classes more likely. Correcting them is tedious, and a correction made after publication disrupts the academic schedule for everyone.

3. Complex coordination between departments

Without a shared system, coordinating between departments, faculty members and administrative units can become chaotic. Miscommunication and delays are common, and each one costs another round of revisions.

4. Difficulty handling changes

Last-minute changes in faculty availability, rooms or student enrolments are hard to manage manually. Updating the timetable quickly and accurately becomes a major task in its own right.

5. Slow, unreliable communication

Informing students and faculty about timetable changes by hand is slow and prone to delay. Making sure everyone is working from the current version takes significant effort, and there is always someone who missed the email.

6. Poor resource allocation and little data to fix it

Allocating classrooms, labs and other facilities is hard without an automated system. Some rooms end up overbooked while others sit underused, and without IT systems there is no easy way to analyse peak usage or spot underused resources. IIM Calcutta saw exactly this before automation: popular courses assigned to undersized rooms, and some rooms consistently empty.

7. Reduced flexibility

Manual systems are rigid. They make it difficult to accommodate special requests, such as personalised schedules for students with special needs, and flexibility in course offerings is often the first thing to go.

8. Inconsistent documentation

Keeping accurate, consistent records of timetables is difficult when each version lives in a different file. Gaps in those records cause problems for future planning and for audits.

9. It does not scale

As universities grow, manual timetabling becomes increasingly impractical and holds back the institution's ability to expand and adapt. At IIM Lucknow, as student numbers grew and courses multiplied, building clash-free timetables after course registration became unsustainable by hand.

10. Student dissatisfaction lands on staff

Errors and inefficiencies in the timetable affect students directly, and can affect their academic performance. When students cannot find an up-to-date schedule, the queries come to the programme office, which adds to the workload in point 1.

What changes when timetabling is automated?

Automation does not remove the programme office from timetabling. It changes what the office spends its time on: less building and checking, more deciding.

  • Clash checking moves to the system. A course clash matrix records which courses students take together, so those pairs are never scheduled in the same slot. IIM Calcutta's system resolved 95% of potential scheduling conflicts.
  • Faculty preferences are collected once, not negotiated by email. At IIM Calcutta, 85% of faculty preferences were incorporated into the final schedule.
  • Registration clashes are fixed automatically. At IIM Lucknow, the system resolved more than 90% of clashes by shuffling students between sections, and the number of students reporting schedule conflicts fell by 60%.
  • Finalisation is faster. IIM Lucknow cut the time needed to finalise its timetable by 50%.
  • Rooms match demand. IIM Lucknow improved classroom utilisation by 20%. Read the IIM Lucknow case study.
  • Faculty calendars update themselves. At IIM Bangalore, teaching slots are blocked automatically in faculty Outlook calendars, with no manual coordination. Read the IIM Bangalore case study.
  • Demand surveys stop being a manual project. Before Registro, NMIMS Mumbai ran demand surveys and generated its course clash matrix by hand, which caused delays. Both are now automated ahead of each bidding cycle. Read the NMIMS case study.

What should a programme office do before automating?

The institutions above offer four practical lessons:

  1. Clean the data first. At IIM Calcutta, data quality was the main implementation challenge. Demand estimates and faculty preferences had to be verified before the system could perform at its best.
  2. Bring faculty in early. Faculty buy-in needed clear communication at IIM Calcutta. Once the system showed it could accommodate most preferences, scepticism resolved quickly.
  3. Connect the timetable to registration. At IIM Kozhikode, bidding and timetabling used to run separately, and conflicts were discovered after bidding closed. Linking the two means bidding outcomes feed straight into the timetable.
  4. Test before you publish. IIM Lucknow generated test schedules to find common clashes before its live rollout, and trained administrators on the dashboard before term began.

Frequently asked questions

Why is manual timetabling so time-consuming?

Because every course, faculty member, room and time slot depends on the others. Staff have to check each combination by hand, and one change can force several others.

What are the most common manual timetabling errors?

Scheduling conflicts, double-booked rooms and missed classes. Most are only discovered after students register or the timetable is published.

Does automation replace timetabling staff?

No. The system generates the schedule and flags edge cases. Staff still make the decisions and can adjust the timetable manually to handle situations on the ground.

Can an automated timetable handle last-minute changes?

Yes. At IIM Lucknow, dynamic adjustment absorbs late enrolment changes with minimal disruption to the published timetable.

How long does implementation take?

Implementing a single process typically takes a few weeks.
