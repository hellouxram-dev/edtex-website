Elective course bidding

What is elective course bidding? How it compares with FCFS and ranked preferences

Elective course bidding is a registration method where every student gets the same pool of bid points and spends them on the electives they want most.

By Arun Korupolu

The short answer: elective course bidding is a registration method where every student gets the same pool of bid points and spends them on the electives they want most. The seats in each elective go to the highest bids. It is the most common method at premier business and law schools because it lets students signal how much a course matters to them, not just whether they want it.

Bidding is not the only way to allocate electives, though. Universities also use first come, first served (FCFS) registration and preference-based ranking. Each has its place, and the right choice depends on your programme size, your seat constraints and how much transparency your students expect. This guide explains all three, in the order most programme offices meet them.

Who is involved in elective course selection?

Enrolments for electives in universities, business schools and law schools involve four groups, and each one feels a badly run process differently:

  • Students, who want a fair shot at the courses that shape their specialisation and placements.
  • Graduate programme offices, who set the rules, run the rounds and answer the queries.
  • Faculty, who need to know their final class sizes early enough to plan sections.
  • Registrar's offices, who record the outcome and enforce credit requirements.

When the method is right and the system works, most of these groups barely notice the process. When it isn't, the programme office spends the first two weeks of term handling complaints.

What is elective course bidding and how does it work?

Course bidding lets students bid for electives through either an open (real-time auction) or a closed (non-real-time) process. In both, each student starts with a pre-allocated budget of bid points. A student who badly wants a popular strategy elective can put a large share of their points on it. A student who is indifferent between two finance electives can spread their points.

Open bidding

Open bidding gives students real-time insight into the demand for each elective while the round is live. They can see how competitive a course is and decide how many points they need to place to secure a seat. This lets students allocate points strategically and win seats in as many of their preferred electives as possible, which improves their overall learning experience.

Open bidding is also the most transparent method. Because students watch the demand move, very few of them question the final allocation. The OP Jindal Global University deployment shows how far this scales: more than 5,000 students across 10+ schools and 45+ programmes bid in a single time-based registration window, and student queries during registration fell by 99%. Read the OPJGU case study.

Closed bidding

Closed bidding does not show real-time demand. Students submit their bids within a set timeframe without knowing what their peers have placed. Once the window closes, bids are processed and seats go to the highest bids.

This takes away the pressure of watching a live auction. The trade-off is that students must be strategic without any feedback on competition, and a student who misjudges demand can lose a seat they would have won by placing a few more points.

What is first come, first served (FCFS) registration?

FCFS is still common at universities with multiple disciplines, where students from several schools take part in the same elective selection. Students win a seat based on the time they select the course on the registration portal: the earlier the click, the better the chance.

It is simple to explain and simple to run. The problem is fairness. Students often feel that FCFS rewards a fast internet connection rather than genuine interest, and those who miss out have to pick from whatever is left. That said, universities can still use FCFS registration data to plan their offerings for the following term.

What is preference-based ranking?

In preference-based ranking, students submit a ranked list of the electives they want. An algorithm then allocates seats using three inputs: each student's preferences, their relative rank (often based on CGPA) and the number of seats available.

Ranking removes the stress of a live auction, and it rewards academic performance. It is less transparent than open bidding, though, because students cannot see how their choices interact with everyone else's until the results are out.

How do the three methods compare?

  • Fairness perception: Open bidding scores highest, because every student starts with the same budget and sees the same demand. FCFS scores lowest.
  • Transparency: Open bidding shows demand live. Closed bidding and ranking show results only at the end. FCFS shows availability but not intent.
  • Signal of intent: Bidding captures how strongly a student wants a course. Ranking captures order but not strength. FCFS captures neither.
  • Administrative load: All three are manageable when automated. Run manually, bidding and ranking create the most work, because results need validating and queries need answering.
  • Best fit: Bidding suits MBA and law programmes with high-demand electives and limited seats. FCFS suits large multi-discipline universities with plenty of capacity. Ranking suits institutions that want to reward academic merit.

Why do business and law schools prefer course bidding?

Premier business and law schools usually have three conditions that make bidding the natural choice: a large number of electives each term, a small number of seats in the most sought-after sections, and students whose electives directly shape their placement story.

In that setting, the method has to be both fair and seen to be fair. Bidding with equal point budgets does that. It also gives the programme office clean demand data, which feeds straight into section planning and the timetable. At IIM Calcutta, Registro has run 21 term-level elective bidding cycles since 2017, covering more than 3,000 students and 500 electives, with manual effort down by 95% and satisfaction consistently above 4.5 out of 5. Read the IIM Calcutta case study.

What should you decide before running your first bidding round?

If you are moving from FCFS or a manual process to bidding, settle these questions with your programme committee first:

  1. Open or closed? Open bidding is more transparent. Closed bidding is calmer. Most premier schools now choose open.
  2. How many points, and do they carry over? Decide whether unspent points roll into the next term.
  3. How will you handle multiple programmes? If students from different programmes compete for the same electives, you may need seat quotas in the first round.
  4. What are the credit rules? Minimum and maximum credits, concentration requirements and prerequisites should be enforced by the system, not checked by hand afterwards.
  5. What happens after the round? Plan your Add/Drop rounds and waitlist movement before the first round opens, not after.

Frequently asked questions

Is course bidding fair to students?

Yes, when every student starts with the same number of points and the rules are published in advance. Open bidding is considered the fairest version because students can see live demand before committing their points.

What is the difference between open and closed course bidding?

In open bidding, students see real-time demand for each elective and can adjust their bids while the round is live. In closed bidding, students submit sealed bids and only see the outcome once the window closes.

Can FCFS and bidding be combined?

Yes. Many schools run a bidding round for high-demand electives first, then open an FCFS or Add/Drop round for the remaining seats.

How long does a bidding round take?

Run manually, a full cycle of bidding, validation and query handling can take weeks. With an automated open-bidding platform, the live round itself can be completed in a single day, with results available immediately.

Which institutions use elective course bidding?

Course bidding is widely used by premier MBA and law programmes. In India, EDTEX's Registro platform runs bidding at institutions including IIM Bangalore, IIM Calcutta, IIM Kozhikode, NMIMS Mumbai, OP Jindal Global University and NLS Bangalore.
