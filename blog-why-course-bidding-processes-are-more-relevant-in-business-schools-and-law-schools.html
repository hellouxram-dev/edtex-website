Elective course bidding

Why is course bidding more relevant in business and law schools?

Why course bidding suits business and law schools: small cohorts, scarce specialised electives and short programmes, as at IIM Calcutta and NLS Bangalore.

By Arun Korupolu

  2. Why does the student profile make bidding a better fit?
  3. What does bidding do for the classroom?
  4. How does bidding help the institution manage scarce resources?
  5. Why do large universities use simpler systems?
  6. What does this look like in Indian business and law schools?
  7. What does a bidding system save the school?
  8. How should you make the case to your committee?
  9. Frequently asked questions
  10. Conclusion

The short answer: course bidding is more relevant in business and law schools because scarcity is built into how they are structured. Cohorts are small, the most valuable electives have limited seats, programmes are short, and students arrive with clear career goals and strong preferences. Bidding lets students show how much a course matters to them, and gives the school a fair, transparent way to allocate seats. Large universities, with more sections, more substitute courses and longer programmes, can usually manage with simpler registration methods.

This post explains the differences in programme structure, student profile and resources that make bidding the natural choice for professional schools, and why large multi-programme universities usually do not need it in the same way. For how bidding works in practice, see what is elective course bidding.

What makes business and law schools structurally different?

Small, intensive cohorts

Business and law schools work on different structural principles from large universities. MBA programmes run relatively small cohorts, and law schools enrol first-year classes of a similar order. Poets&Quants has compared the big and small MBA cohorts at top business schools. At this scale, the most popular electives hold only a fraction of the batch, and the allocation problem becomes acute. We look at the numbers in how to run elective bidding across multiple graduate programmes.

Large universities serve thousands of students across dozens of majors, and most courses have multiple sections and broader capacity. Scarcity is less pronounced there, because an alternative section or a similar course is usually available.

Specialised, high-value electives

Business and law schools offer specialised electives taught by industry experts, practitioners and well-known faculty. Picture a private equity elective taught by a practising investor, or a litigation seminar led by a senior advocate. Courses like these cannot easily be replicated, and that is what justifies a competitive allocation method.

Specialisation also means students have clear, career-specific needs. An MBA student aiming for investment banking will prioritise different electives from one focused on entrepreneurship, which creates natural demand imbalances that bidding resolves efficiently. Leland sets out how programme structures differ across top business schools.

Why does the student profile make bidding a better fit?

Career-focused students with a lot at stake

MBA and law students are typically older, more career-focused, and have made a substantial financial investment in their education. That profile matters in three ways:

  1. Strong preferences: they know which courses align with their goals.
  2. Willingness to strategise: given what they have invested, they engage seriously with the mechanism. Schools take the design of that mechanism seriously too: Wharton replaced its course auction with a computerised matching process.
  3. Informed decisions: professional experience helps them make considered choices about where to spend their points. Economists have studied course bidding at business schools closely too; see this paper on course bidding at business schools.

Time-compressed programmes

Undergraduates have several years to sample disciplines. MBA students have two, and much of the first year is taken up by required core courses. That leaves only a handful of elective slots to build a specialisation and a placement story. With so few slots, each allocation carries real weight, and the method has to be both fair and seen to be fair.

What does bidding do for the classroom?

Bidding creates commitment. When students have to decide where to spend a fixed budget of points, they arrive in the courses they chose on purpose. The competitive process fills courses with students who value them, which makes for livelier discussion and better peer learning.

How does bidding help the institution manage scarce resources?

Better resource allocation

Bidding gives schools clear data on student preferences. They can see which courses deserve extra sections, where curriculum gaps exist and how to plan faculty load. In expensive programmes, that directly affects the return on every faculty hour. EDTEX co-founder Arun Korupolu sets out the institutional benefits in his article on cross-programme elective bidding.

Managing scarcity fairly

Undergraduates can often resolve a clash by taking a course in a later semester. Business and law programmes have rigid sequencing and limited time, so that option rarely exists. Bidding offers a transparent mechanism for managing scarcity that students accept as fair, and law school registrars publish their rules in detail so students know how it works. See Northwestern Law's registration instructions for an example.

Why do large universities use simpler systems?

Large universities typically use first come, first served registration or priority based on class standing. The University of Chicago's pre-registration FAQ shows how such a process is run. These methods work adequately there because:

  1. Multiple sections are available for popular courses.
  2. A broader curriculum offers substitute courses.
  3. Longer timeframes let students take a course in a later semester.
  4. Less specialisation means any single course choice matters less to career outcomes.

When large universities do face scarcity, they tend to use lotteries or priority registration, as The Harvard Crimson explains for Harvard's general education courses. These are simpler than the bidding systems used in business and law schools. Undergraduates are also often still exploring disciplines, so the stakes of any one course are lower.

What does this look like in Indian business and law schools?

At IIM Calcutta, Registro has run 21 term-level elective bidding cycles since 2017, covering more than 3,000 students and 500 electives, with manual effort down 95% and student satisfaction consistently above 4.5 out of 5. Read the IIM Calcutta case study.

Law schools face the same conditions. NLS Bangalore runs elective bidding for its BA LLB (Hons.), LLM and other graduate degrees on a single platform, with cross-programme access to electives across law, business, policy and international relations, and real-time updates on bids and seat availability. Read the NLS Bangalore case study.

What does a bidding system save the school?

Manual or legacy elective allocation costs the programme office weeks after every round: validating bids in spreadsheets, correcting transcription errors, answering students who cannot see how seats were decided, and waiting on final numbers before faculty can plan sections. A bidding platform such as Registro course bidding removes most of that work.

  • Time: IIM Rohtak has run 15 bidding cycles on Registro since 2018, and a complete elective bidding cycle now runs end to end in under 60 minutes. Read the IIM Rohtak case study.
  • Queries: at OP Jindal Global University, where students can see the allocation process in full, student queries during registration fell by 99%. Read the OPJGU case study.
  • Planning data: every round records what students actually want, so the school can add sections for high-demand courses, drop or repurpose undersubscribed electives, and put faculty and rooms where the demand is.
  • A clash-free timetable: IIM Kozhikode runs Registro course bidding and timetable scheduling together, so bidding outcomes flow straight into conflict-free timetables. Read the IIM Kozhikode case study.

Students gain too. They know their allocation as soon as the round closes, and they can build a set of electives that fits their career plans. At both IIM Calcutta and NMIMS Mumbai, student satisfaction with the bidding process has been rated above 4.5 out of 5. Read the NMIMS case study.

The rules should be configurable as cohorts grow: open (real-time) and closed (batch) rounds, bid-point allocation, cross-programme access to electives, and prerequisite and degree requirement checks. At the far end of the scale, OPJGU runs a single registration window for more than 5,000 students across more than 10 schools and 45 programmes.

How should you make the case to your committee?

  1. Measure the current cost. Count the weeks your office spends per round, the queries received and the corrections made after results are out.
  2. Count the missed signals. Note electives dropped for low demand, sections added late and clashes found after allocation.
  3. Set the outcomes you expect. Time per cycle, query volume, student satisfaction and on-time timetable publication.
  4. Plan the rollout. Implementation of a single process typically takes a few weeks, so a first cycle can run within one term.
  5. Agree the policy first. Points, rounds, quotas and credit rules should be settled before configuration starts. See how to establish an elective course bidding policy.

Frequently asked questions

Why don't large universities use course bidding?

Most do not need it. More sections, more substitute courses and longer programmes mean FCFS or priority registration usually works well enough.

Is course bidding used in law schools?

Yes. Law schools share the conditions that suit bidding: small classes, specialised electives and limited seats. NLS Bangalore runs bidding across its undergraduate and graduate law programmes.

Does bidding reward students with more points?

Not when it is designed well. Every student in a programme starts with the same budget, so outcomes reflect how much each student values a course.

Can undergraduate programmes use bidding?

Yes, where electives are scarce and specialised. NLS Bangalore includes its integrated BA LLB (Hons.) students in the same bidding process as its graduate programmes.

How quickly can a bidding round be completed?

With an automated platform, a complete cycle can run in under 60 minutes, as at IIM Rohtak, with results available immediately.

Does a bidding system replace the programme office?

No. It removes repetitive validation and query handling so staff can focus on policy, faculty and students.

Conclusion

Course bidding fits business and law schools because they combine structured scarcity, high student investment and a sharp career focus. Small cohorts, specialised electives and short programmes make a fair, data-rich allocation method necessary, while large universities can usually rely on simpler systems.
