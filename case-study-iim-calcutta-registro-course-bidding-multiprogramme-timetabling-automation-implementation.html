Overview

IIM Calcutta implemented Registro Timetabling to replace a manual process that had become unsustainable as courses and faculty numbers grew. The system is built on demand estimation data, a course clash matrix and faculty preferences. It cut scheduling time by 70%, resolved 95% of potential conflicts and incorporated 85% of faculty preferences into the final timetable.

Before automation, the scheduling team placed courses into time slots by hand, trying to balance student demand, clash avoidance, room availability and faculty preferences at once. As complexity grew, this led to conflicts and to students who could not take the courses they wanted because of poor slot allocation.

More on the product: Registro Timetabling.

What manual scheduling could not sustain

Manual timetabling meant constant back-and-forth between faculty, administration and students, juggling demand predictions, clash avoidance and faculty constraints with no single system. As the number of courses and faculty grew, the result was more conflicts, poorly used classrooms and dissatisfied students.

The goals of automation were clear: schedule more efficiently, place courses according to actual demand, prevent clashes and double bookings, include faculty preferences without manual negotiation, and adjust quickly when enrolments or availability change mid-term.

Where every term fell short

  • Weeks spent placing courses into slots without data on actual demand
  • No clash matrix; course conflicts found only after schedules were published
  • Faculty preferences collected by hand, applied inconsistently and often overridden
  • Popular courses placed in rooms too small for their enrolment
  • Some classrooms overbooked while others stayed empty
  • No way to re-schedule quickly when enrolments or faculty availability changed
  • Growing student dissatisfaction from avoidable timetable conflicts

How Registro builds the schedule

Registro Timetabling draws on three data sources (demand estimation, the clash matrix and faculty preferences) and a constraint-based optimisation engine that brings them together into a schedule with as few conflicts as possible.

Demand estimation model

Historical enrolment data predicts course sizes, so popular courses get suitable rooms at well-attended times.

Course clash matrix

Common course pairings identified so that courses often taken together are never scheduled at the same time.

Faculty preferences

Faculty submit preferred teaching windows and constraints; the system accommodates as many as possible.

Constraint-based optimisation

Balances clash minimisation, room matching, faculty preferences and the spread of time slots.

Dynamic re-scheduling

Quick adjustments when enrolment or faculty availability changes, without starting from scratch.

Classroom assignment

Courses matched to rooms on predicted demand, ending mismatches between class size and capacity.

Lessons learned

Data quality was the main implementation challenge. Accurate demand estimates and up-to-date faculty preferences were essential, and early inconsistencies had to be cleaned and verified before the system performed well. Faculty buy-in needed clear communication; once the system showed it could accommodate most preferences, scepticism faded quickly.
